# Case 1: Pepperfry.com — Marketing to Manage Customer Experience

## Source

`9. Pepperfry com-2.pdf` (Ivey W17332, Singh, Puri, Roy, 2017).

## Key Facts from the Case

- Setting: September 2016. Pepperfry positioned as India’s pioneering online furniture and lifestyle e-tailer.
- Leadership: Ashish Shah (co-founder, COO); Kashyap Vadapalli (CMO); Ambareesh Murty (co-founder, CEO).
- Stated CMO goal in case: help 20 million customers create beautiful homes by 2020.
- Financial signals: 13 to 15% margin improvement in 2015; product margins of 35 to 50%; turnover of about INR 10 billion in 2015; annual revenue growth of more than 250% consistently since 2013; expected break-even by Q1 of fiscal 2018-19; sold a product every 25 seconds; shipped to over 1,000 cities; in 2014 more than 70% of online transactions came from India’s top 10 cities, indicating consumer reluctance to buy furniture online.
- Strategic problem: furniture is non-standard and not impulse; potential customers explored the portfolio about 15 to 20 times before deciding; average purchase took 2 to 4 weeks; average order value about INR 12,000.
- Studio Pepperfry: online-to-offline hybrid experience centres designed for product experience and expert advice rather than direct selling.
- Marketing initiatives: 360-degree integrated campaign positioning Pepperfry against home-made furniture (noise, dust, time and budget overruns, design and functional flaws); marketing at airport lounges; tie-ups with HomeStop; virtual reality tours of Studio Pepperfry.
- Funding: in September 2016, Pepperfry led a capital raise of about US$160 million, twice that of competitor Urban Ladder. Funds went to studios, logistics, technology, and marketing.
- Industry context: Indian online retail forecast to reach US$55 billion by 2018 from US$14 billion in 2015 and US$220 billion by 2025 in gross merchandise value.

## Where the Case Plugs In

### Product as Offering and Product Levels (`01_introduction_and_product.md`)

Pepperfry is offering more than furniture:

- Core benefit: confidence and convenience in furnishing the home online.
- Actual product: curated furniture and lifestyle catalogue.
- Augmented product: Studio Pepperfry experience, expert advice, virtual reality tours, delivery, and post-purchase service.

### Customer Experience and Adoption Process

Consumers explore the portfolio 15 to 20 times and take 2 to 4 weeks to decide. Adoption-rate factors apply:

- Relative advantage: cheaper than custom-made; fewer hassles; better design.
- Compatibility: solves Indian consumers’ existing concerns about home-made furniture.
- Complexity: online furniture is hard to evaluate; reduced through Studio Pepperfry and VR tours.
- Trialability: experience centres provide trial without forcing purchase.
- Observability: airport lounge marketing and 360-degree campaigns.

### Place and Distribution

The case provides direct material on online-to-offline hybrid retail and the strategic role of Studio Pepperfry. Useful when the Place chapter is added later.

### Promotion and IMC (`04_promotion_imc.md`)

The 360-degree campaign against home-made furniture is a textbook example of integrated communication aligned to one positioning.

## Likely Questions

1. How does Pepperfry use customer experience to overcome reluctance toward online furniture?
2. Apply product levels to Pepperfry.
3. How does Pepperfry’s 360-degree campaign illustrate IMC?
4. What role does Studio Pepperfry play in the adoption process?
5. Should Pepperfry expand offline studios further? Justify using product, place, and promotion logic.

## Recommended Answer Structure

- Define product as a value offering.
- Apply product levels.
- Use the adoption-rate framework.
- Show IMC across digital, airport lounges, studios, and VR tours.
- Recommend balancing studio expansion with logistics, technology, and brand investment.

# Case 2: When Your Brand Is Racist — Cork Beverages and Overseer Whiskey

## Source

`10. When Your Brand Is Racist.pdf` (HBR fictionalized case study and commentary; Joseph C. Miller, Michael A. Stanko, Mariam D. Diallo).

## Key Facts from the Case

- Setting: a U.S. brewing and distilling company called Cork Beverages, headquartered in Nashville. Senior brand manager Shawn Lewis (Black) is responsible for Overseer Whiskey. CEO is Jim Worth. Head of PR is Angela Howell. Other actors in board discussion: Carla, Eric, Becca, Chrystal.
- Brand history: Overseer Whiskey was started by Samuel Vernon, a distiller and plantation owner in Tennessee, in the early 19th century. According to brand lore, the brand was named after an enslaved man whom Vernon promoted to foreman of his cornfield. Throughout the 1800s and well into the 1900s, ads depicted a smiling Black man in field clothes. Cork bought the brand in the 1950s and over 40 years downplayed plantation imagery while making Overseer a U.S. household name.
- Trigger: Black Lives Matter protests; many brands are being called out for racist names or histories; Google searches for `Overseer` are increasing; risk of viral exposure of the brand’s troubling past.
- PR draft proposed by Angela: Cork acknowledges Overseer’s history is closely tied to racism, says past changes were not enough, is evaluating further measures, commits to diversity, equity, and inclusion, and announces a US$3 million donation to organizations supporting and engaging the Black community.
- Customer research: 42% of whiskey drinkers know what an overseer is; most customers do not associate the brand with slavery; customers in the Southeast and Midwest reportedly have no problem with the name or history.
- Naming and rebranding options on the table:
  - Keep the name (do nothing).
  - Tweak the name to `Seer` or `Chattanooga Seer` (testing showed best scores among renames).
  - Pull product under the existing Element brand to leverage its built-up equity.
  - Build an entirely new brand around the same taste profile.
- Decision pressure: CEO and Carla want to move fast and announce plans soon; the board may listen seriously to Shawn’s recommendation.
- Commentary perspectives: experts argue against half-measures and image tweaks, support bold action, and reference examples like Washington Redskins, Eskimo Pies, and Ben and Jerry’s response after George Floyd’s killing.

## Where the Case Plugs In

### Brand Equity, Identity, and Image (`03_branding.md`)

- Brand equity: Cork has built strong consumer awareness for Overseer over decades; recognition is a financial asset, but the brand’s associations are now ethically toxic.
- Brand identity vs image: company-intended meaning has shifted, but the historical name and lore still encode a problematic association.
- Brand associations: many consumers do not link Overseer to slavery; consumer ignorance is not consumer endorsement.
- Brand elements: name, label imagery, and brand lore are core to the controversy.

### Brand Development Strategy

The four options map directly to brand development decisions:

| Option in case | Brand development concept |
|---|---|
| Keep the name | No change; carries reputational risk. |
| Rename to Seer or Chattanooga Seer | Modified existing brand identity; reduces but may not remove association. |
| Move product under Element brand | Brand consolidation/extension under a different existing brand. |
| Build a new brand around the same taste | New brand creation. |

### Ethics and Social Responsibility in Product/Brand Decisions

The case is a clear test that brand strategy is not only about marketing performance; it includes responsibility for harmful associations regardless of consumer awareness levels.

## Likely Questions

1. Should Cork Beverages keep, rename, consolidate, or replace Overseer Whiskey? Justify.
2. Apply brand identity vs brand image to Overseer.
3. Is the US$3 million donation enough? Use brand strategy and ethics arguments.
4. How should rebranding be communicated to consumers in the Southeast and Midwest who did not see a problem with the brand?
5. Compare options using brand development logic.

## Recommended Answer Structure

- Briefly summarize the brand and the trigger.
- Explain identity-image gap and the role of harmful associations.
- Map the four options to brand development strategy.
- Discuss trade-offs: equity preservation, ethics, customer reaction, cost, brand trust.
- Recommend a specific course with clear reasoning, transparency, and follow-through.

# Case 3: A Fast-Food Company Considers Dynamic Pricing — Burger and Bites

## Source

`11. A Fast-Food Company-2.pdf` (HBR fictionalized case study and commentary by Elie Ofek; reactions by Philip Daus and Dave Burwick; based on the HBS case `Dynamic Pricing at Wendy’s: Where’s the Beef?`).

## Key Facts from the Case

- Setting: Burger and Bites, a fast-food chain. Marcus Patel is the leader presiding over a meeting facing an unexpected and volatile inflation crisis.
- Key actors: Laura Ramirez (CFO) shows accelerating input costs; Jay (technology / menu screens) advocates for dynamic pricing; Amira raises consumer backlash and equity concerns; Araceli Martinez is a Texas franchisee owning 15 restaurants; John proposes price caps and using dynamic pricing only for promotions/discounts; Elena is a technology ethicist and marketing professor invited for perspective.
- Existing capability: digital menu screens and an upgraded app already deployed.
- Strategic options on the table:
  - Across-the-board price increase.
  - Shrinkflation: smaller portions, fewer fries, thinner patties, dried instead of fresh ingredients.
  - Real-time dynamic pricing based on input costs, demand patterns, and other factors.
  - Personalized, app-based dynamic pricing for tech-savvy customers.
  - Price caps to prevent surge.
  - Using dynamic pricing only for promotions and discounts, not surge.
- Industry analogies cited: airlines, hotels, ride-hailing services like Uber, and Disney theme parks all adjust prices to demand.
- Risks raised in case: backlash about surge during a time of anger over corporate profit-seeking; transparency, equity, and consumer trust concerns.
- Expert recommendations:
  - Philip Daus (Simon-Kucher): dynamic pricing is not the right tool for Burger and Bites; instead, use it through delivery apps where consumers expect dynamic fees, and use personalized app promotions based on purchase history and geolocation.
  - Dave Burwick (Spindrift CEO): Marcus is not yet ready to decide; he must consider impact on value proposition, brand equity, and trust; recommends supplier negotiations, scrutinizing internal overhead, freight, packaging, and delaying nonessential capex.

## Where the Case Plugs In

### Pricing Strategies (`02_pricing.md`)

- Dynamic pricing strategy and ethical questions.
- Price increase causes: inflation.
- Personalized pricing using device, location, and history.
- Price adjustment vs price change.
- Cost-based pricing pressure under accelerating input costs.

### Brand Equity and Trust (`03_branding.md`)

Dave Burwick’s commentary explicitly warns that dynamic pricing must be evaluated for its impact on value proposition, brand equity, and trust. This connects pricing back to brand strategy.

### Promotion and IMC (`04_promotion_imc.md`)

Philip Daus’ suggestion to use app-based personalized promotions through purchase history and geolocation links pricing to direct/digital marketing within the promotion mix.

## Likely Questions

1. Should Burger and Bites adopt dynamic pricing? Justify.
2. Compare across-the-board price increases, shrinkflation, dynamic pricing, and personalized app pricing.
3. What ethical risks does dynamic pricing carry for a fast-food brand?
4. How should pricing decisions be communicated to preserve brand equity?
5. Diagnose the situation using cost-based, value-based, and competition-based pricing logic.

## Recommended Answer Structure

- Identify the underlying problem: inflation plus consumer price sensitivity.
- Apply cost-based pricing (break-even pressure), value-based pricing (perceived fairness), and competition-based pricing (industry behaviour).
- Evaluate dynamic pricing benefits and risks using fairness, transparency, and trust.
- Recommend selective use (delivery apps, app-based promotions) instead of surge-based dynamic pricing.
- Add supplier and operational levers as alternative cost relief.

# Case 4: Kumar Food Corporation — Building Shagun Beyond Western India

## Source

`12. Kumar Food Corporation-2.pdf` (Ivey 9B19M140).

## Key Facts from the Case

- Brand: Kumar Food Corporation (KFC) markets papads and pickles under the brand name `Shagun`, a traditional Indian word meaning auspicious start, good omen, or gifts on occasions like engagement, marriage, or birth. The logo is red.
- Tagline evolution: initially `Swadhar pal k liye` (taste in every moment); later changed to `Real spice, real taste` for papads and `Where quality is tradition` for pickles.
- Brand-name strategy: Kumar deliberately diverged from the spice segment’s common practice of using founder names (e.g., MDH, Pravin Spices, Ravi Spices) to choose a name that would be universally accepted.
- Distribution: hybrid B2B and B2C model. Operating from Pune; supplying Western Maharashtra, Marathawada, and Khandesh. 16 distributors deliver to canteens. 14 distributors deliver to about 500 retail outlets in Pune. An exclusive distributor handles modern trade retailers. Some seasonal distributors are added during peak periods.
- Brand-building activities mentioned in case: three-foot banners, stickers, exhibitions, and free samples.
- Government policy context cited: food processing classified as a priority sector for bank loans; 100% compensation for loss and capital allowed; full duty exemption on units in Export-Processing Zones (EPZ); 100% duty exemption in EPZ for imports; Start-Up India initiative launched on August 15, 2015 by Prime Minister Narendra Modi.
- Competition mentioned in case:
  - Everest Spices: trendsetter for over 45 years, presence in roughly 1,000 towns, about 400,000 outlets, reach of around 20 million households, strong international presence.
  - Mahashian Di Hatti Private Limited (MDH): legacy of 88 years, known for uniform taste and quality with fully automated processes.
  - Catch Spices (Dharampal Satyapal Group): turnover of INR 65,000 million per year, entered spices in 1987, expanded into many categories, perceived as a premium F&B brand.
  - Local players in local stores benefit from lower prices.
- Aspiration: Kumar wants to make Shagun a national brand and increase its value to INR 200 million.
- Open questions in case: should KFC expand geographically, add new product categories, or restrategize the distribution model?
- India consumption context (Exhibit 6): vegetarian/non-vegetarian percentages by state, with Rajasthan, Haryana, Punjab, and Gujarat showing high vegetarian shares.

## Where the Case Plugs In

### Product as Offering, Product Mix, and Strategic Adjustments (`01_introduction_and_product.md`)

- Shagun is a portfolio of papads and pickles. Adding new categories increases product mix width or length.
- Kumar must decide whether geographic expansion or category extension creates more value.

### Branding (`03_branding.md`)

- Shagun is a deliberate brand-name choice that breaks the founder-name convention to support universal acceptance.
- Brand elements: name, red logo, taglines, banners, exhibitions, and sampling.
- Conditions for stronger brand equity: building awareness beyond Western Maharashtra, strengthening associations through consistent positioning, and converting trial samples into repeat purchase.

### Competitive Strategy (`05_competitive_strategies.md`)

- Established competitors: Everest, MDH, and Catch.
- KFC behaves like a regional challenger or market nicher, depending on framing.
- Competitive options: focus by region, product type, quality, or distribution; or full-frontal expansion into national markets.

### Distribution

- The case provides B2B/B2C hybrid distribution detail useful for the future Place chapter.

## Likely Questions

1. How should KFC build Shagun into a national brand?
2. Apply branding concepts (identity, elements, equity) to Shagun.
3. Should KFC expand geographically, add product categories, or restrategize distribution? Justify.
4. Position Shagun against Everest, MDH, and Catch using competitive strategy frameworks.
5. What are the trade-offs of using a hybrid distribution channel for a regional brand aiming for national growth?

## Recommended Answer Structure

- Define product mix width and length and explain implications of adding new categories.
- Use brand identity and brand elements to assess Shagun.
- Diagnose competitive position relative to Everest, MDH, and Catch.
- Recommend phased expansion: deepen Western India distribution, then enter adjacent regions, then add categories.
- Mention risks: stretching resources thin, losing regional strength, weak brand recall outside core territory.

# Case 5: Ariel #ShareTheLoad — Integrated Marketing Communication Campaign

## Source

`13. Ariels #ShareTheLoad-2.pdf` (Berlin Business School / HBP TB0638, by Dharminder Kumar Batra).

## Key Facts from the Case

- Brand: Ariel, P&G’s premium laundry detergent brand in India.
- Setting: Pankaj Srivastav (a pseudonym), marketing head of Ariel India, reviewing monthly sales reports and #ShareTheLoad campaign performance. He is concerned the campaign message could lose impact and consults branding/media teams.
- Campaign launch year: 2015. Continued running for several years, including in 2021.
- Core message: doing laundry is not necessarily restricted to a particular gender; the campaign promotes gender equality through household chores.
- Behavioural metric in case: in 2015, 79% of men thought household chores were a woman’s job; this dropped to 63% in 2016 and 52% in 2019. Reduction was attributed to the campaign’s success.
- Reach data: `Sons #ShareTheLoad` garnered 73 million views nationally; reach of approximately 80% in the desired target group; about 45,000 conversations; sentiment 98% positive and neutral.
- Campaign extensions in case:
  - 2016 `Dads #ShareTheLoad`: focused on fathers as role models so children learn gender-equal household behaviour.
  - Equal sleep edition / 71% of women sleep less due to household work.
  - SON-Day: every Sunday boys are encouraged to learn household chores using a rap song.
  - #HamperOfEquality: a curated box sent to prospective customers with items for each household member to remind them to share the load.
  - Tinkle comic partnership: stories of how Tinkle’s favourite characters share the load.
  - Holi tie-up with Whirlpool, Big Bazaar, and Metro Cash and Carry: a crash course for men on doing laundry.
- Strategic objectives given to the agency: create recall; launch and create buzz around the latest campaign thought; drive behavioural change through PR, events, social media, influencers, marketing associations; sustain the buzz through pan-India brand collaborations and concept-driven events.
- Media approach: 360-degree integration across all media vehicles for impact and recall.
- Business outcomes from Exhibit 5 in case:
  - Ariel ranked among Brand Equity’s 10 Most Trusted Brands (#7 vs #44).
  - Unaided Brand Awareness up 13 percentage points pre-STL vs after-STL.
  - Consideration up 21 percentage points pre-STL vs after-STL.
  - Offtake (sales) at index 125 vs prior period.
  - Several other brands joined the household-equality cause.

## Where the Case Plugs In

### IMC (`04_promotion_imc.md`)

- Direct illustration of integrated marketing communication: a single core message communicated consistently across television, social media, PR, partnerships, retail, comic books, and consumer-engagement boxes.
- Demonstrates AIDA: attention through socially provocative messaging, interest through emotional storytelling, desire through aspirational social meaning, and action through behaviour change and brand preference.
- Demonstrates promotion goals: informing about gender inequality in chores, persuading men to share the load, and reminding consumers through repeated campaign editions.

### Brand Activism and Brand Equity (`03_branding.md`)

- Ariel’s rise into Brand Equity’s 10 Most Trusted Brands is a measurable brand equity outcome of the campaign.
- The case is a clear example of brand purpose translating into commercial impact.

### Push vs Pull Strategy (`04_promotion_imc.md`)

- Ariel mainly used pull strategy through consumer-facing communication and emotional engagement, supported by retail partnerships at point of purchase.

## Likely Questions

1. How does #ShareTheLoad illustrate IMC in action?
2. What were the main objectives of #ShareTheLoad and how were they achieved?
3. Evaluate the campaign using AIDA and the goals/tasks of promotion.
4. How does brand activism relate to brand equity in this case?
5. How should Ariel sustain the campaign without losing impact, given Pankaj’s concern?

## Recommended Answer Structure

- Define IMC and 360-degree communication.
- List campaign editions and media vehicles to show coordination.
- Use AIDA and informing/persuading/reminding logic.
- Quote specific behavioural and brand metrics from the case (79% to 52%, 73 million views, sentiment 98%, awareness +13, consideration +21, sales index 125, Brand Equity rank from #44 to #7).
- Recommend campaign refresh ideas grounded in the case mission while preserving the core message.
